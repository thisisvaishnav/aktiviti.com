20

In the app today.

  1. Process Inspector.

    1.9.0

    Click any process for everything about it: its code signature, hardened runtime and sandbox, its architecture, its parent process, launch agent and command line, its CPU time and memory against its peak, its files, disk and ports, and its child processes. Its app’s version, bundle and Info.plist too, and all of it exports as CSV.

  2. Install with Homebrew.

    1.8.0

    brew install, the way developers install their menu bar tools. Same app, same license key.

  3. A command for Terminal.

    1.8.0

    aktiviti status, aktiviti top, aktiviti ports and the rest: what Aktiviti sees, as a table or as JSON for scripts.

  4. A free trial.

    1.8.0

    Three days of every feature before you buy, with no account and no card.

  5. An Agents tab for Claude Code, Codex and the rest.

    1.7

    Every coding agent running on your Mac: the project it works in, what it is doing, how long it has run, and everything it started, with Quit for the session and all of it together. For Claude, Codex, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Antigravity, Grok, Ollama and OpenCode, how much of each plan is left, when it resets and whether you are on pace, for every account signed in on the Mac.

  6. 90 days of history, or a year.

    1.6

    Keep 30 days, 90 days, 6 months or a year, chosen in Settings, so “what was heavy in July” has an answer, for you and for your AI assistant.

  7. Your own alerts.

    1.6

    “Tell me when Chrome passes 8 GB for five minutes”, or when the Mac stays above 90 °C. Or mute an app that always runs hot.

  8. Export your history as CSV.

    1.6

    Your apps by day, your Mac by hour, and the readings every five minutes, as three files that open in any spreadsheet.

  9. Make it yours.

    1.5

    Chart colors, the font and weight of the numbers, comfortable or compact spacing, and how far back the charts go, on a Customize page with a live preview. Exported pictures follow.

  10. Readings turn red under strain.

    1.5

    In the menu bar, a reading turns red while the CPU or GPU is at 90%, memory is under pressure, the disk is nearly full or the Mac runs hot. Asked for by a customer; a switch in Settings → Layout → Menu Bar.

  11. A Neural Engine tab.

    1.5

    How hard the Neural Engine is working and which apps are using it, in a tab of its own, in the menu bar and on the Overview. For anyone who runs AI models on their Mac.

  12. An MCP server, so your AI assistant can ask Aktiviti first.

    1.4

    Claude, Codex, Cursor and any assistant that speaks MCP can ask what is running, which app is behind a number, and whether something is safe to quit, before they touch anything. Off until you turn it on. Aktiviti itself sends nothing anywhere.

  13. Aktiviti in your language.

    1.3

    German, French, Spanish, Italian, Swedish, Japanese, Chinese and more, ten languages at the start, in the words your Mac already uses.

  14. Quit any app from the dropdown.

    1.2.1

    Hover over an app, click the close button, confirm. Force quit if it doesn’t respond. Containers get a Stop button the same way.

  15. Fan control.

    1.2.1

    Each fan on automatic or at a speed you choose. Back to automatic when Aktiviti quits.

  16. Docker containers.

    1.2

    Every container with its memory, CPU, network and ports, inside the project it belongs to.

  17. Browser tabs and extensions.

    1.2

    Chrome, Safari, Firefox and the rest split into tabs, extensions, graphics and the browser itself.

  18. Global keyboard shortcuts, and Shortcuts app actions.

    1.2

    Open the dropdown or the window from any app. Get a reading or the busiest app in your own shortcuts.

  19. A compact menu bar.

    1.2

    For 13‑inch screens. Settings → Layout → Menu Bar → Size.

  20. A customizable menu bar.

    1.1

    Every readout can be its own item, in any order, with its own style.

Next

6

Coming, in this order.

  1. 1

    A storage map: what takes your disk space.

    Every folder sized, biggest first, with a plain label: macOS, an app’s data, or yours. Caches, old Xcode files and forgotten node_modules are named for what they are and what happens if they go.

  2. 2

    Safe cleanup of caches and old build files.

    Caches, Xcode leftovers, Docker images and old node_modules, one at a time, each with a line on what it is and a confirmation. No “clean everything” button. Nothing that belongs to macOS is ever touched.

  3. 3

    Drive health.

    How worn your SSD is, how much has been written to it, and whether it reports a problem, for the built-in drive and any external drive that reports it. A warning as soon as a drive says something is wrong.

  4. 4

    Disk activity for each drive.

    Reading and writing for each connected drive, not only the total, with a filter on the Disk tab’s chart. Every connected volume listed with its free space.

  5. 5

    Mac Health.

    One line on how your Mac is doing, with the cause named when something is off: “All calm”, or “Running hot: Xcode has used six cores for twelve minutes.” Under it, the checks behind it: battery, drive, temperature, memory and free space. One click copies the full picture as text.

  6. 6

    A watchdog for runaway dev processes.

    A dev server left behind, a tool stuck at full CPU, a process using more and more memory for hours. Aktiviti notices, tells you, and can stop it for you if you allow it. Never macOS, never an app with a window open.

Later

18

After Next. No order yet.

  1. USB devices.

    Everything plugged in, by name, with the speed it is connected at next to the fastest it supports, so a fast drive on a slow cable or port stands out, and the power the Mac gives it.

  2. Desktop widgets.

    Memory, disk, battery and your busiest apps on your desktop, in view without opening anything.

  3. Free up memory.

    One place that names what is holding memory without being used: apps left in the background for hours, dev servers nobody is using. Each with what quitting it gives back, and a Quit button. Not a number that drops and fills up again a minute later.

  4. Background tasks: what starts with your Mac.

    Every background task on your Mac, the launch agents and daemons, listed under the app that installed it, with whether it is running and what it uses. Tasks left behind by apps you deleted are marked. Switch one off, and back on, with a click. Nothing that belongs to macOS is touched.

  5. Trends in Worth a Look.

    What has changed, not only what is happening now: “Your disk will be full in about 40 days.” “The battery drains faster than last week, and Chrome is behind it.” “Slack uses twice the memory it did.”

  6. Where the battery went since you unplugged.

    “I unplugged at nine and I’m at 60%. Where did it go?” Each app’s share of the charge used since the cable came out, and how fast the battery is draining now, on the Battery tab.

  7. Fan curves.

    Fans that follow a temperature you pick: quiet while the Mac is cool, faster as it warms up. Or full speed for 15 minutes before a build or a render, then back. Above a safe limit the fans always go back to automatic.

  8. Your own limit for free disk space.

    “Tell me when the disk has less than 10 GB free”, at the amount you pick.

  9. Push notifications to your phone.

    Every alert sent to a webhook, so it works with ntfy, Pushover, Slack or Home Assistant. For a Mac that runs on its own. Quiet hours included.

  10. An internet speed test.

    Download, upload and latency on the Network tab, kept per Wi‑Fi network, so you can tell your Wi‑Fi from the website.

  11. IP addresses, Wi‑Fi signal, and an alert when the connection drops.

    This Mac’s address on your network and your public address on the Network tab, each copied with one click, with your Wi‑Fi’s signal, band and link speed beside them. The public address is looked up only when you ask. Get a notification when the connection drops and when it is back.

  12. Reports.

    Your Mac this week on one page: which apps used the most battery, memory, network and disk, what changed since last week, and what Aktiviti warned you about. Save it as a PDF or CSV, or let your AI assistant read it.

  13. Record a build or a slow moment, second by second.

    Press Record before a build, a render or a slow moment, and Stop when it’s over. See what peaked, when, and which app was behind it. Export it as CSV, or let your AI assistant read it.

  14. A Prometheus exporter.

    For Grafana and homelab dashboards. Off by default, and this Mac only unless you say otherwise.

  15. Clean up Docker’s disk.

    From Disk Use on the Containers tab: remove unused images, stopped containers, unused volumes or the build cache, one kind at a time. Each asks first and says how much it frees. Volumes hold data, so only those no container uses.

  16. OrbStack machines.

    Each Linux machine you run in OrbStack with its memory and CPU, beside the containers Aktiviti already shows.

  17. A shortcut that quits the heaviest app.

    Press it anywhere, see the app and its number, press Return. Never macOS, Finder, or an app with unsaved work.

  18. A notch display.

    A panel that opens from the notch on MacBooks that have one. Off by default.

Not planned

5

Asked for, looked into, not coming. Here so nobody waits.

  • Watching other Macs from this one.

    Aktiviti watches the Mac it runs on. For another Mac that runs Aktiviti, its command answers over SSH from any computer, and so does its MCP server, for an AI assistant on another computer. A fleet of Macs in one window is a different app.

  • Ask Aktiviti.

    A chat inside Aktiviti, answered by Apple Intelligence. Your own assistant already asks Aktiviti through its MCP server, and the aktiviti command answers in Terminal, so a second, smaller assistant inside the app would answer the same questions less well.

  • A battery charge limiter.

    macOS has one in System Settings → Battery since 26.4. A limiter that gets it wrong leaves a laptop uncharged before a flight, and that is not a risk a monitor should take. Aktiviti will show the limit macOS has set instead.

  • Memory per browser tab.

    Chrome doesn’t tell macOS which tab a process belongs to, and one process can hold several tabs. Aktiviti splits a browser into tabs, extensions, graphics and the browser itself, which answers “is it my tabs or my extensions?”.

  • A “Free up RAM” button.

    On a Mac it empties a cache that refills within minutes, and everything is slower until it does. macOS gives that memory back the moment an app needs it. Aktiviti shows what holds memory and lets you quit it, which is what the button was for.
