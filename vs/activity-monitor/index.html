Quick answer

Aktiviti is an activity monitor and process inspector for Mac: CPU, GPU, memory, temperatures, fans, disk, network and battery in the menu bar and in a window, every app with its helper processes counted in, a Process Inspector with any process’s code signature, parent, command line, CPU time and memory peak, up to a year of history, a notification when an app misbehaves, dev servers and their ports, Docker containers, coding agents and AI plans, a volume slider for every app, and a aktiviti command and MCP server, paid once. Activity Monitor is the process monitor that comes with macOS: one row per process for this second, live graphs, and the Sample and spindump tools, which Aktiviti leaves to it.

Where Aktiviti pulls ahead

01

One row per app, not forty

Aktiviti adds each helper to the app that launched it, so Chrome is one row with one figure for memory, CPU, energy, disk and network. Activity Monitor lists about 1,000 processes on a working Mac, dozens of them called Google Chrome Helper.

02

It remembers

Aktiviti keeps up to a year for every app, so what drained the battery this afternoon still has an answer tomorrow. Activity Monitor shows this second, plus 12 hours of energy on a laptop.

03

It tells you

Aktiviti sends a notification when an app keeps the CPU busy, keeps growing in memory, or hammers the disk or network, and names the app. Activity Monitor waits to be opened.

Everything Aktiviti does

One app, paid once, no subscription.

  • A tab per subsystem. Overview, CPU, Memory, Disk, Network, GPU, Neural Engine, Battery, Sound, Bluetooth and Projects, plus Containers while Docker runs, each with its live figures and the apps behind them.

  • A menu bar you arrange. Every readout can be its own menu bar item: CPU, memory, GPU, network, disk, temperature, fan speed or battery, as a value, a graph or both, ⌘-dragged into any order. Behind it, a dropdown with every metric, the busiest apps, and a tab for each metric.

Click any process for the app it belongs to, its code signature, Team ID and entitlements, its parent process and launch agent, its full command line, the processes it started, and the CPU time, memory, open files and listening ports it has used since it started. An app’s version, bundle ID and Info.plist too. Copy it as text, save it as CSV, or get the same from aktiviti process in Terminal and over MCP.
  • Up to a year of history. CPU, memory, energy, disk and network from the last hour to the last year, with the apps that used the most, ranked hour by hour for the last two days. Keep 30 days, 90 days, 6 months or a year.
  • Alerts for misbehaving apps. A notification when an app keeps the CPU busy, keeps growing in memory, or hammers the disk or network.
  • Your coding agents, and what is left of your plans. It cannot quit anything by itself: Aktiviti asks you first. Off until you turn it on.
  • The aktiviti command. A command for the terminal that answers from the running app: the apps using the most, ports and dev servers by project, coding agents, what is left of each AI plan and up to a year of history, as a table or as JSON. Quitting and stopping ask first. It comes with the app, or with Homebrew.

Common questions

Does Aktiviti replace Activity Monitor completely?

For the everyday question, which app is doing this, yes. For sampling a process, spindumps and system diagnostics, no: Activity Monitor does those and Aktiviti does not try to. Keep both: Activity Monitor stays in Utilities for the rare day you need it.

Is Activity Monitor free?

Yes. It comes with every Mac, in Applications → Utilities. Aktiviti is paid once: $15 for life during the launch and $39 after, with a 14-day refund; or $9 for a year of updates.

Why does Activity Monitor show so many processes?

Modern apps split their work across helper processes: a browser runs one for each tab and extension. Activity Monitor lists each one; Aktiviti adds them up under the app that launched them.
